Problem, objectives and scope

A single component failure can interrupt end-to-end traffic until routing adapts. The objectives are to compare recorded RIPv2 and OSPF behaviour on identical addressing and topology, verify alternate and restored routes, and present the evidence with its limitations. The work covers a four-router Packet Tracer ring, a recorded link failure, R4 failure and link restoration. Three additional link timings are illustrative estimates. No new routing protocol was developed.

Calculations and source policy

Per-link reduction = (RIP − OSPF) / RIP × 100. Time ratio = RIP / OSPF. Four-link arithmetic average = sum of the four supplied values / four links. Reduction of averages = (RIP average − OSPF average) / RIP average × 100. These are descriptive comparisons; three link inputs are Assumed (estimated – not measured). They do not establish statistical significance or repeatability. No standard deviation, error bars or loss percentages are fabricated.

Different ways to learn a route

RIPv2

Distance vector

Routers exchange distances to networks. RIP uses hop count, supports up to 15 hops, and uses 16 as infinity. Periodic and triggered updates help distribute changes [1,3].

OSPF’s event-driven link-state processing is consistent with the shorter recorded link-failure OCRT. This experiment does not isolate timer, SPF, ARP or simulator contributions, so it cannot prove the cause of the difference.

06 / METHOD & LIMITATIONS

Evidence is useful when its limits are visible

Recorded procedure

  1. Use the same ring, addressing and traffic pair for the protocol comparison.
  2. Verify the route, then start continuous PC ping.
  3. Trigger the failure and start the external stopwatch.
  4. Stop at the first reply followed by another consecutive successful reply.
  5. Record the route, preserve the ping evidence, then restore the component.

Exp A: PC1→PC2, R1 G0/0 shutdown. Exp B: PC1→PC3, R4 off. Exp C: R1–R2 restored. The saved files were inspected read-only; no experiments were rerun for this dashboard.

What the data can support

R1–R2 was timed in Packet Tracer; the values for the other three links are estimates assumed from the R1–R2 observations and the expected behaviour of the protocols, because OCRT differs from run to run and those links were not individually timed.

Exp B compares unlike earlier/later runs. The opposite-LAN paths are equal-cost via R2 and R4. Forwarding already via R2 could explain OSPF’s no-loss observation, but that is an unverified hypothesis.

No completed ping summaries or repeated-trial datasets are available. The four-link descriptive average includes Assumed estimates. All four links need repeated timing before an experimental average is meaningful.

The quoted guide paragraph’s identical-conditions statement does not establish a direct comparison for Exp B’s unmatched runs. Restoration’s TTL-change interval is separate from outage OCRT.

Findings and next steps

The recorded R1–R2 observation is ≈26 s for RIP versus ≈7 s for OSPF, a 73.08% shorter OCRT in these single runs. Both protocols returned traffic through the alternate ring path and later restored the preferred route. The four-link descriptive means, including three Assumed links, are 25.75 s and 7.00 s; the reduction of these means is 72.82%, with no statistical claim.

Next: individually time every link, repeat each scenario at least ten times, capture full ping summaries and automated timestamps, and compare controlled matched runs on emulated or real routers. The dashboard is an evidence presentation and illustrative replay; its animated paths are not simulation evidence.
